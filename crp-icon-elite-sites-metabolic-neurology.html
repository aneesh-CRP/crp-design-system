What made it work

  • Lp(a) testing in house. We settled the eligibility criterion before screening rather than during it.
  • Cardiology referral base. The practices sending us ASCVD patients are the same ones we have worked with for years.
  • One coordinator queue. Every identified patient has a single owner, so nobody is worked twice or dropped.
  • Both sites eligible. Philadelphia and Pennington drew from separate catchments on the same protocol.

What it means for a study footprint

  • At 52 randomized against a 15-patient allocation, this one site carried the work of roughly three and a half average sites.
  • That is three fewer site activations, three fewer sets of regulatory packets, and three fewer monitoring routes for the same patient count.
  • The 80% screen-fail rate is normal for an Lp(a) outcomes trial. We absorbed it rather than passing it back as a recruitment problem.

Recruitment

We do not buy patients per study. We screen a database we already own.

191 referring practices covering 1.5 million patients, and a rules engine that re-checks our own 37,000 against every open protocol.

Referring provider network

  • 191 practices, 892 named providers across cardiology, endocrinology, primary care, neurology and dermatology in PA and NJ — 1.5 million patients in their panels.
  • Direct EMR access at many of them, so a diagnosis-driven candidate list for a new protocol comes back in a day rather than a month.
  • A provider portal launched July 2026 lets a referring office send a patient and get the result back without phoning us.
  • Health information exchange through HSX and NJHIN for clinical record retrieval before a patient arrives.

Our own database

  • 37,887 patients in our EDC, built over 30 years of trials at the same two addresses.
  • Every open protocol is scored against every patient by an eligibility engine, so a new study starts with a candidate list instead of an empty funnel.
  • Prior inclusion and exclusion results carry forward. A patient cleared for one lipid trial does not get re-worked from zero for the next one.
  • One coordinator queue with a single owner per patient, so nobody is worked twice, dropped between channels, or called by two people in a week.
This is the part that transfers. The network and the database do not reset when a study closes, so the second protocol in a therapeutic area is always cheaper to enroll than the first.

How we would operate inside the program

Two locations, one operation — so you contract once and monitor one set of processes.

Philadelphia, PA and Pennington, NJ. Roughly 50 miles apart, drawing from separate catchments, run off a single system.

What is shared across both sites

  • One contracting entity and one budget process. A master agreement covers both sites; there is no second negotiation.
  • One EDC instance and one set of SOPs maintained to ICH-GCP E6 (R3), so a monitor sees the same source structure at either address.
  • One coordinator queue and one investigator roster. Seven investigators cover both sites; patients move between them when a protocol needs it.
  • One regulatory function submitting to the same standing IRBs for both locations in parallel.

What we are asking the program for

  • A master agreement executed up front, so protocol-level start-up is a budget conversation and nothing else.
  • Metabolic placement now, where our delivery record is established.
  • Neurology placement in migraine and early Alzheimer's, where we have the investigators, the pool and the sponsor history but need the volume.
  • A named contact on each side and a quarterly review against agreed enrollment commitments.
Capacity to take work now. 24 protocols are currently enrolling across both sites, with coordinator rotation across studies and no site running at ceiling. Two sites operating in parallel means an ICON study can open at both addresses under a single activation.
